That's the pose.

CAP8 was designed against the CDRL3 loops of 23 human MS autoantibodies, on the reasoning that CDRL3 is homogeneous in structure where CDRH3 is not, so one 8-mer scaffold might fit many targets. In this model it lies across the antigen-binding site rather than inside one loop, touching six residues: Tyr31 (CDRL1) and Trp99 — the tryptophan of this antibody's CDRL3 — on the light chain, and Trp144 (CDRH1), Glu168 and Tyr170 (CDRH2) and Pro211 (CDRH3) on the heavy chain. The design work found the aspartates at positions 7 and 8 doing most of the hydrogen bonding here, four polar contacts in all.

Measured, it bound the anti-GlialCAM autoantibody with a Kd of 0.294 mM, against 2.61 mM for an unrelated cyclic 8-mer and 4.82 mM for linear CAP8, and cut autoantibody detection of its epitope by 69% on a Western blot. Binding to a non-pathological influenza antibody was no different from the controls. All of this is preliminary: inhibition was partial, the control peptides cut the signal 27% and 5% on their own, the affinity difference sits at p = 0.047, and of the three antibodies in the design set this was the one whose 100 ns trajectory did not pass the stationarity tests. What this is and isn't

Real docking searches six degrees of freedom plus the peptide's own flexibility, scored by a force field. Here the depth is locked to the plane of the published complex, so you are solving three — two of position, one of rotation. Contacts and clashes are counted honestly, over every heavy atom within 20 Å of the site: a contact is a peptide atom within 4 Å of the antibody, a clash is any pair closer than 2.6 Å.
