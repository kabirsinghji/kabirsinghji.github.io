That's the pose.

In the published model CAP8 sits against six residues of the antibody's combining site — Tyr31 and Trp99 on the light chain, Trp144, Glu168, Tyr170 and Pro211 on the heavy chain. The peptide was designed against the CDRL3 loops of 23 human MS autoantibodies, on the reasoning that CDRL3 is structurally homogeneous where CDRH3 is not, so one scaffold can plausibly fit many targets.

Measured, it bound the anti-GlialCAM autoantibody with a Kd of 0.294 mM — about an order of magnitude tighter than linear or scrambled cyclic controls — and cut autoantibody detection of its epitope by 69% on Western blot. Binding to a non-pathological influenza antibody was indistinguishable from controls. Inhibition is partial, not complete, and the controls showed modest effects of their own, so selectivity needs firmer evidence before in vivo work. Read the paper →

What this is and isn't

Real docking searches six degrees of freedom plus the peptide's own flexibility, scored by a force field. Here the depth is locked to the plane of the published complex, so you are solving three — two of position, one of rotation. Contacts and clashes are counted honestly, over every heavy atom within 20 Å of the site: a contact is a peptide atom within 4 Å of the antibody, a clash is any pair closer than 2.6 Å.
