Over the summer, my first peer-reviewed paper was published in the European Journal of Medicinal Chemistry!

Sep 2026

Many current multiple sclerosis drugs work by suppressing whole populations of immune cells. That helps many patients, but it can come with serious side effects, like infections, much like how chemotherapy can affect healthy cells in the body. What if we could selectively target the harmful antibodies themselves instead? In MS, some antibodies mistakenly target myelin, the protective coating around nerves. One of their targets, a protein called GlialCAM, was recently linked to Epstein-Barr virus: antibodies made against the virus can cross-react with it. Rather than design a separate drug for every harmful antibody (which is very inefficient!), we asked whether one molecule could block many of them. What we found: 🔬 We designed CAP8, a ring-shaped (cyclic) peptide just 8 amino acids long, to plug the binding site of 23 different human MS autoantibodies. 💻 In the molecular dynamics simulations I ran, CAP8 stayed stably in the binding pocket in most cases. 🧪 In vitro, CAP8 bound the anti-GlialCAM autoantibody about 9 times more tightly than a control peptide and cut its binding to its target by 69% (vs. 27% for the control). 🛡️ It showed no preferential binding to a normal human antibody against the flu, an early hint that it could leave healthy immunity alone. These are early results, and the paper is upfront about what comes next: safety, dosing, delivery, and testing in animal models. That road ahead is what excites me most. Translational medical research means carrying an idea from a computer model to the lab bench and, one day, to patients. This project, funded by NJIT's Collaborative Early Research Translation (CERT) grant, gave me my first real look at that journey. It's the kind of work I hope to build my career around as a future physician-scientist. A massive thank you to my PI, Vivek Kumar, to Corey Heffernan Ph.D. for leading this work, to Owen West, Joseph Dodd-o, Meera J., Aditya Baradi, Katelan Rybak and Abdul Lateef, and to our collaborator Stella Elkabes at Rutgers New Jersey Medical School. 📄 Link to the paper in the comments! Now that I've settled into my senior year, I wanted to look back on an incredible summer at Imperial College London! I had the privilege of spending the summer in Prof. Stefano Angioletti-Uberti's group, supported by an Undergraduate Research Opportunities Programme (UROP) bursary from the Department of Materials. Nature has spent millions of years evolving proteins that bind their targets remarkably well. My project used protein language models (AI trained on millions of natural protein sequences) to design new, smaller binders that mimic these natural ligands more efficiently. I worked with BAGEL, the group's open-source protein design package, and refining its parameters for this new use case was one of the most rewarding parts of the summer! A huge thank you to Stefano Angioletti-Uberti for his mentorship, and to the Department of Materials for making this possible! Outside the lab, I made the most of my time in the UK. I visited University of Oxford, University of Cambridge, and University of Southampton, where I had the pleasure of meeting Prof. Jonathan W Essex, and I connected with so many members of the Sikh community along the way. Thank you to everyone who welcomed me! On a lighter note, a good bagel turned out to be harder to find in London than a good binder, so we had to generate our own (the photo may have had a little AI help 🥯). Why molecules and manuscripts

I stumbled into research. It started as a box to check on the way to medicine. In my first semester I joined a peptide lab as a math major, surrounded by chemists, with no clue what I was getting myself into. I took it seriously anyway, and it became the career I want.

Not everything goes to plan. When our lab moved to Houston, our team kept the work going from Newark.

On the ambulance, medicine is about the next ten minutes. In the lab, it is about the next ten years. Translational medicine sits between the two, and that is where I want to work: treating patients, and helping build treatments that reach far more than one person.

I stumbled into history too. While studying abroad in Sweden, I met Satnam Singh, a Danish historian. I read his work and wanted to add to the field myself.

The tools are getting extraordinary. Soon we will design molecules faster than we can test them, and the hardest questions will not be computational: who gets these therapies, who patients trust, how we care for people at the end of life. History helps me think about those.

Sikh history stays with me too, as a pursuit of its own.
